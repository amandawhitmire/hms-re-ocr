Another explanation for differential localization by individuals is based around

genetic differences between Ocenebra in different zones. It is possible that individuals in
the Chthamalus zone have genetically-increased tolerance for desiccation or temperature
stress, and thus localize to the Chthamalus zone to avoid competition for resources or
exposure to predators. If this is the case, individuals simply consume prey according to its
availability within their zone. Hughes and Taylor (1997) found that dogwhelks (Nucella
lapillus) from different populations have slightly different shell morphologies. This
genetically transmitted trait corresponds with the amount of wave exposure the
population experiences. Whether Ocenebra at Hopkins Marine Station is composed of
two or more genetically variable sub-populations with one population localizing to the
Tetraclita zone, and one to the Chthamalus zone remains to be examined.

Variation in Ocenebra feeding behavior also occurs. Ocenebra were observed
attacking Tetraclita in two ways: by drilling into the side of the barnacle, and by prizing
open the opercular plates. These two methods of handling prey were observed at
approximately equal frequencies both in the laboratory and in the field. In the laboratory
tanks, however, individuals never drilled all the way through the barnacle shell wall.
Several individuals had an established pattern of drilling first, and then consuming the
Tetraclita by prizing open the opercular plates. This pattern remained relatively
consistent over several feeding cycles. In the field, the frequency of success after drilling
through the side of the Tetraclita shell is unknown. Two barnacle shells were found with
holes drilled completely through by Ocenebra, evidence that it is possible to drill through
the side of the shell. Ocenebra may be learning how to feed more efficiently by handling
