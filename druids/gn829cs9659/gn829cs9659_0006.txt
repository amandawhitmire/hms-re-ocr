Fibers of a shorter length relative to the long fibers are also
depicted in fig. 2b. These run between the long fast fibers and the
adjacent bone at about a 45° angle. They extend only along the
spine half of the muscle bundle. These fibers are very slow (see
also below) and were not used for the present study. However, this
region of the muscle deserves histological examination, because

structures closely resembling muscle spindles in higher vertebrates

were observed at the spine end of the slow fiber region. This is

noteworthy, because muscle spindles in teleosts have been described
only in one case (Maeda, et al, 1983), and that structure was very
primitive, containing only one intrafusal muscle fiber.

Properties of twitches and tetani:

Fig. 3a illustrates that the long fibers respond to a single
shock (between 3-20 volt/cm field strength) applied along the length
of the entire muscle with a fast twitch, 10-15 ms to peak, and a

rapid relaxation, 20-30 ms, at 17-20°c. (The term twitch, as used in
this paper is defined as a rapid contraction.) This speed of

contraction and relaxation is comparable to one of the fastest
vertebrate muscles described, cat extraocular muscles at 37°c
(Bach-y-Rita & Ito, 1966). Long fibers are much faster than frog
(Rana) twitch fibers which follow a time course of 30-50 ms to peak
contraction and 100 ms to relax at room temperature (Katz, 1966).
