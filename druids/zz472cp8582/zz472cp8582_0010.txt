Finally, although Pycnopodia does eat C. canaliculatum,
studies on its diet have shown that this snail is not a preferred
prey, while its close relative C. ligatum is a major source of
nutrition for the seastar (Herrlinger, 1983). The latter species
has an almost identical escape response to that of C. canaliculatum,
with the important exception of the hypobranchial secretion (Harrold,
1981 Herrlinger, 1983). Thus, it seems evident that the intensity

of the C. canaliculatum escape response is directly correlated to the
Pycnopodia's dietary preference; this correlation between actively
escaping from predators and the abundance of prey that ended up in
the diet has been extensively studied using different predator-prey
relationships (Feder, 1963, 1967; Phillips, 1976; Dayton et al.,
1977; Moitoza and Phillips, 1979; Harrold, 1981, 1982; Herrlinger,

1983; Watanabe, 1983).

Secretion Production Experiments:

The question of whether the noxious substance is derived form
the diet or by endogenous synthesis seems to be decided in favor of
endogenous synthesis. The ability to produce the hypobranchial
secretion does not depend on the food that C. canaliculatum feeds on
(at least over a two week period when secretion was repeatedly
elicited), but rather on the time it has to regenerate the substance.
