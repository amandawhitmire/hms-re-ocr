DISCUSSION

Behavioral Experiments:

One problem I encountered with these studies was the
reluctance of the Pycnopodia to naturally forage after the C.
canaliculatum in aquaria. Attempts were made to induce the seastar
into moving in the general direction of the snail placed in the tank
but to no avail. Therefore, the technique using individual tube
feet described above was used to simulate an attacking seastar.
With a nutural predatory event there would be more variability in
timing and persistence. The described behavior represents a
hierarchy of responses to sustained attack. It would be of great
interest to observe the behavior in the field to document its form,

success, and other consequences.

I noticed that for all thirty snails tested the secretion of
the exudate was a last ditch technique used to escape. The C.
canaliculatum were very reluctant to release the product. Also, I
often found the snails holding on the substrate with the anterior par
of their feet, while the ventral side of the posterior part of their
feet wrapped up onto the dorsal surface of their shells. This has
been observed in C. ligatum as well (Harrold, 1981), and is
apparently used to maintain a mucus layer on the shell.
