The results from the uncaging experiments performed in this study support the theory of

cooperativity at the IP3 receptor. Release of relatively high intracellular concentrations of IP3
(100nM) by flash photolysis results in a rapid increase in intracellular [Ca2+]. Taking cooperative
binding into consideration, it is concluded that the undocumented portion of the rising phase due
to the speed limitations of the imaging equipment (see Materials and Methods) represents the
lower foot portion of the sigmoidal curve characteristic of cooperative binding.

Gradual rising phase following flash photolysis suggests regeneration

An averaged cellular response to flash photolysis of caged IP3 exhibits a rapid increase in
[Ca2+]; following uncaging, but a noticeably slower rise (1-2 seconds) to peak Ca2+ levels (Fig. 4).
This gradual rise to maximum intracellular [Ca2+] suggests a regenerative process at the level of
IP3 receptor activation. Rather than achieving peak cytosolic Ca2+ levels immediately, the peak is
attained through repeated activation of IP3 receptors. Researchers have theorized that
submicromolar concentrations of Ca2+ can cause IP3 receptors to reach threshold by altering the
receptor's binding affinity for IP3, thus activating the receptors and releasing additional Ca2+ into
the cytosol. This theory of a Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release at the IP3 receptor [Finch et al., 1994] can
explain the apparent regenerative process observed following uncaging of IP3.

Flash photolysis of caged IP3 abolishes wave propagation

The working model of Ca2+ wave propagation in neuronal cells suggests an active process
underlying wave propagation. This process may involve an interaction between cytosolic Ca2+
and IP3 receptors at the wave front [Wang et al., 1995]. The model is based on the assumption
that IP3 receptors are distributed throughout the entire cell, and not localized to a particular area.
