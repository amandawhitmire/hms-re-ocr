FIGURE LEGENDS

Figure 1. Comparison of relative shell heights for the four species tested.
The shells are divided into low(Climatula), middle(C. pelta, C. digitalis)
and high(A. mitra) relative heights.

Figure 2. A. Diagram indicating variable shell thickness in limpet shells.
The cross-section shows that the margins and apex are thickened relative
to the muscle scar. B. Diagram of the underside of a limpet shell,
illustrating the horseshoe-shape of the muscle scar.

Figure 3. A. (From MacClintock, 1967). Diagram showing the orientation
of shell layers in a limpet shell. The layer labelled "m" is the
myostracum(muscle scar layer). B. Cross-section of the shell at the level
of the muscle scar, showing that the myostracum originates on the inner
surface of the shell where the "scar" appears. A crab-like force would
produce tension at the inner surface(the myostracum). P=Prisms,
CL = Crossed - lamellar .

Figure 4. Bending caused by crab-like forces at the muscle scar. The
calcium carbonate shell would tend to fracture first along the inner
surface since the shell is weaker in tension than compression.

Figure 5. Photograph of limpets used in study, showing relative height
differences. From left to right: Climatule, C. digitalis, A. mitra, C. pelte.
Figure 6. Limpet shell crusher used to fracture shells. A. Side view,
showing a loaded shell situated between two metal plates. The plates are
attached to the large wood blocks. B. Front view. The wood block at the
left provides a substrate for the shell, preventing the shell from slipping
to the left. The right side of the device is wedged against some object to
prevent sliding to the right. C. Side view, showing two aluminum braces
attached to the upper block. The braces prevent the upper block from
sliding over the shell(to the left or right).
