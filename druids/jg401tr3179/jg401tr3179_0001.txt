Introduction

Macrocystis Pyrifera, giant kelp, is a brown algae that forms a surface.
canopy along the coast of North America from Baja California to San

Francisco, in South America, southern Africa, and southern Australia (Abbott
and Hollenberg 1976) It displays a typical Laminariales life cycle, with
alternation of generations from a microscopic, sexual gametophyte stage to a
large, asexual sporophyte stage (figure 1). Special fertile fronds called.
sporophylls, located near the base of the kelp, release free-swimming spores
which attach to the substrate and form gametophytes. After about a month
of development (Deysher and Dean 1986), male gametophytes release
biflagellate sperm which fertilize an egg attached to the female. The
sporophyte begins growth attached to the female gametophyte. (see North
1974, Dayton 1985 for more details) Kelp forests dominated by Macrocystis
pyrifera have long been a subject of great interest because of their economic.
and ecological importance. Since the advent of SCUBA, many researchers.
have designed field experiments attempting to model dynamics of the kelp
forest. This has proved to be an extremely difficult task due to the complex,
interactive, and unpredictable nature of this environment.

To understand the kelp forest, one must begin with an understanding
of Macrocystis itself. Although studies are being carried out every day in
kelp forests around the world, thousands of questions are still unanswered.
Many investigators here in California have chosen to narrow down the
questions even farther, addressing the microscopic stages of the Macrocystis
life cycle. What are the dynamics of gametophyte recruitment and survival?
What factors, physical or environmental, control mortality? Many methods.
for study have been developed, a few questions have been answered, but
many more remain. For example, if Macrocystis is fertile year round.
