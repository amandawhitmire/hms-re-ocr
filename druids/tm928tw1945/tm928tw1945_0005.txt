Discussion

The results shown in Figures 3, 4 and 5 indicate that algal-microfauna
associations at Pt. #2 are characterized by having more species of animals
and higher diversity indices values than those at Pt. Pinos. If we accept the
idea of Pt. #2 being a control, a natural environment that is not polluted as
there is reason to believe Pt. Pinos is, then this data suggests that the entirety
of Pt. Pinos is somehow changed. Differences in diversity indices and numbers
of species found in the samples taken around the point show areas of changed
community structure, which correlated with the distribution of sewage effluent
imply that parts of Pt. Pinos are polluted. Accepting the hypothesis that lower
values for species diversity indices and numbers of species when correlated
with sewage effluents indicate pollution damage, Figures 3, 4 and 5 imply
that all of Pt. Pinos has suffered pollution damage.

However, to test the validity of the data statistical methods were em-
ployed. Significant differences (p<.001) were found between the "polluted"
and "unpolluted" areas at Pt. Pinos and between "polluted" areas at Pt. Pinos
and areas at Pt. #2. for the numbers of species of animals found associated with
the algae. There was not a significant difference between the "unpolluted"
group and Pt. #2 for this criterion, suggesting that numbers of species of
animals found in a group of samples can reveal changes in a community.

For the diversity indices significant differences (p<.001) were found
between all comparisons. This would suggest that diversity indices vary too

much from sample to sample to serve as effective indicators. However, by
comparing the "unpolluted" group with itself and the Pt. #2 group with itself,
insignificant differences (p.2) were found for both diversity indices. This
indicates that the indices are consistent within a given geographical area
such as Pt. Pinos and that they do serve as indicators of change in a community.
