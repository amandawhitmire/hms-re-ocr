The problem is compounded when the species in question have

microscopic, free swimming larva, or release their gametes directly.
into the water. Both of these cases have a high potential for

migration, even if the adult forms are stationary. Unfortunately, it is
impossible to directly observe the extent of such migration. An
alternative to direct observation is to study the large scale population
genetics of a species, and infer gene flow and migration rates from
the genetic data. It is possible to estimate both the extent of
population subdivision and migration from allelic frequencies (Slatkin,
1987.) The data gained from biochemical population studies is also
useful in that it allows examination of gene flow on a evolutionary
time scale, instead of simply examining present trends. Jeff Mitton,
Carl Berg and Katherine Orr used allozyme data to estimate the extent
of gene flow between different subpopulations of the Caribbean Queen
conch Strombus gigas (Mitton et al, 1989), and B.J. Davis et al used
allelic frequencies to estimate gene flow between Pacific Coast
populations of the fish Oxylebius pictus (Davis et al 1981.) Both
studies found evidence of high gene flow between subpopulations, with
significant but unpredictable differences in local allele frequencies.

rates, and 2) to attempt to gain additional insight into the
