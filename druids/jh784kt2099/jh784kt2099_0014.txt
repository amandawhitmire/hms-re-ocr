population of Semibalanus cariosus i sampled at Half Moon Bay was
almost indistinguishable from the Tetraclits populations at all four
polymorphic loci studied. It seems unlikely that this is merely a
coincidence, and there are several alternative possibilities to explain.
this result. One possibility is that the two groups are really only one.
species, and there is extensive gene flow between them. This seems
extremely unlikely, since there are important morphological
differences between the two groups, and they brood at opposite times
of the year (Morris, Abbot, Haderlie, 1980 p517,519). A second
explanation is that there are strong selective forces operating on both
species which maintain the allele frequencies at the same levels. This

has some plausibility; both species occupy the same ecological niche,
the lower intertidal zone, and the species look superficially alike.
Their geographical ranges meet up with each other with little overlap.
(figure 1), and it seems likely that they face similar environmental
conditions. In theory, genetic drift can be a powerful force in changing.
allele frequencies, even in large populations, so long as the alleles are
selectively neutral and the time period in which change can occur is
great (Hartl and Clark, chapter 2, 1989.)
