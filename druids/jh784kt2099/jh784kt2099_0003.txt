There have been considerably fewer cases where natural selection
could be positively identified as a force for maintaining variation.
when no clines were present. Ward Watt's work on Calies butterflies
is one such case (Watt, 1984), and Ronald Burton and Marcus Feldman's
work on the copepod Tigriopus is another (Burton and Feldman, 1982.)
Both of these groups have correlated the function of a specific enzyme
with differential fitness among individuals carrying alternate alleles
for the loci studied. These cases are dwarfed by the number studies in
which variation is observed and the forces, if any, maintaining it have
yet to be discovered. Among these are the many geographic population.
studies in which no obvious patterns of allele frequencies were found.

Many of the projects involving marine organisms on the west coast of
North America fall into this category (see Levinton et al, 1976, Davis
et al, 1981, Smith et al, 1987, Grant, 1988.)

Although there still exists controversy over the meaning and
importance of allelic variation to evolution, this variation has
recently proven itself useful in the study of the population dynamics
of marine organisms. Traditional methods of studying population.

subdivision, migration and gene flow by direct observation are

difficult, if not impossible, to perform on small marine organisms.
