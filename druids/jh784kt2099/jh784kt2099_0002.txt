Introduction

Since the advent of starch gel electrophoresis in the late 1960s,

a great deal of genetic variation has been discovered in countless
populations of organisms. Plants, insects, reptiles, mammals,
invertebrates, birds, and many other groups have all been studied and
shown to harbor vast amounts of allelic variation within their

populations. Discovering why this variation exists and its importance
to evolutionary and population biology has been a more difficult and
controversial task. Most explanations can be separated into two

categories: those that invoke natural selection as a force responsible
for maintaining genetic variation, and those that believe most of the
variation observed is selectively neutral. These positions are

commonly known as the selectionist and neutralist theories,
respectively. A great deal of time and effort has been spent on trying.
to prove or disprove both of these positions.

Many studies have sought to prove that environmental factors, and
hence natural selection, are responsible for maintaining allelic
polymorphisms. Many of these studies have been performed on marine
organisms, and among the more significant are Williams, Koehn and
Mitton's work on the American eel, Anguilla rostrats (Williams et al,
1972), Koehn, Milkman, and Mitton's work on the blue mussel, Mytilus

edulis (Koehn et al, 1975), and Power's group's work on a fish.

Fundulus heteraclitus (Powers, 1986.) All three of these studies
