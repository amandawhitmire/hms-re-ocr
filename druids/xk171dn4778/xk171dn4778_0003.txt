Putnam (1964) studied the dispersal of young Crepidula adunca from Tegula funebralis and
found that while 150-200 young are released from a female on one host, eight is the
maximum number of Crepidula ever seen on one Tegula shell. She concludes that young
must therefore be moving to new hosts. In preliminary experiments to determine impetus
for movement, Putnam (1964) discovered that when removed from their hosts, "adult males
are attracted by Tegula funebralis, but even more strongly by female C. adunca. In contrast,
the newly hatched spat do not appear to be strongly attracted to either T. funebralis or to
older C. adunca, male or female." She concluded that juveniles move about at random and
are only "weakly attracted to either T. funebralis or older C. adunca; this attraction is clearly
present in older individuals."

Though Putnam (1964) states that attraction of males to females is clearly present in
older individuals, she fails to give substantial proof to support this claim. This study was
conducted to determine if and how often Crepidula adunca moved from its host snail, and

the impetus for movement.

Materials and Methods.

Tegula and Crepidula distribution in the intertidal.

In order to determine abundance and distribution of Crepidula in the intertidal, I
sampled organisms from a transect perpendicular to Agassiz beach in the Hopkins Marine
Life Refuge. Along this transect I collected organisms from 10 quadrats. Tidal heights for

each quadrat were measured with a surveyor's transit and stadia rod, relative to the predicted

level of low tide.
