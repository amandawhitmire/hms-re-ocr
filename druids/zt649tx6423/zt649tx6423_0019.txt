If this

theory holds true for C. scabra, then the thicker-shelled rider
C. scabra do not need as much algae as the lighter-shelled
nonrider C. scabra because, while they are expending the same
amount of energy for shell material annually, the thicker-shelled
riders have lower metabolic costs because they grow and maintain
a proportionally smaller body weight.

One could expand on this hypothesis in two ways. First, if
it were shown that rider C. scabra growth rates are food limited,
one could further hypothesize that the decreased food
availability is only limiting body size and shell production
continues at a constant rate (as Palmer, 1980, suggests). The
distribution of shell in relatively thicker layers would simply
be due to the body failing to expand beneath it. On the other
hand riders may inherently lay down thicker shells, perhaps
necessitated by an increased exposure to wave action and
predation because they are removed from their camouflage and the
protection of rock protrusions. If this is true then the body
size of the riders is limited by the internal volume of the
shell, and an increase in food availability on the L. gigantea
shells would not increase the riders' body sizes.
