DISCUSSION

The subpopulation of C. scabra that lies within the one
meter range of L. gigantea distribution is larger at lower
intertidal sites than at higher sites (see Fig 1b). This trend
is opposite to that previously notes by Morris, et al,
which the general population of C. scabra are larger at higher
intertidal sites. This reversal in C. scabra size distribution

within this one meter range can probably be explained by the
presence and size distribution of L gigantea in the same range
(see Fig. 1a). The L. gigantea bulldoze much of the area, which
greatly affects the distribution and population of C. scabra in
the area. If a particular C. scabra is able to resist the
bulldozing force of a L gigantea, it is more likely to be found
in this range than if it were not able to resist dislodgement.
W. G. Wright (1977) showed that homing species, including C_
scabra, can sometimes resist forces in excess of the L.

gigantea's bulldozing force but only if the force is applied when
the C. scabra is on its homesite. Limpets without homesites
could not resist the bulldozing at all. Assuming, then, that 1.)
The better fit the C. scabra is to its homesite, the better it
can resist dislodgement, and 2.) The larger the L. gigantea and
C. scabra. the larger respective forces they can exert on each
other in a bulldozing match, the reversal in C. scabra size
distribution within this range can be explained by the fact that
larger L. gigantea were found at the lower heights.
