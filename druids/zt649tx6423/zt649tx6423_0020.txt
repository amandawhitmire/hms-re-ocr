Because C. scabra never leave the shell of the L.

gigantea, they are likely to never forage over long distances.
C. scabra in the surrounding area, however, can forage over long
distances and because of inter- and intra-species competition for
the local algae, they are more likely to need to forage over long
distances to get a sufficient food supply. Breen (1971) showed
that Collisella digitalis moved greater distances from its
original site when food availability was low and the same may be
true for C. scabra. Calow, 1974, and Denny, 1980, have both
indicated that locomotion in gastropods can be a significant
metabolic cost. Calow (1974) reports locomotion costs to be as
high as 26% of total food intake and if rider C. scabra don't
incur this metabolic cost then they can exist on less algae and
still maintain the same respiration rate, which I have shown them

to do.

While rider C. scabra show differences in shell growth,
foraging area, density of population, and perhaps in distances
travelled compared to nonriders of the same species, L. gigantea
appear unaffected by the existence or nonexistence of riders on
their backs. The major change incurred by the L. gigantea with
the addition of a C. scabra is the loss of the algal coat that
would otherwise be covering its shell (see fig. 5). This
